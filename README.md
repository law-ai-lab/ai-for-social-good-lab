# AI for Social Good Lab

Independent public-interest AI research and development by **Rie Nakahara**.

We investigate how older adults can access everyday necessities while retaining dignity, autonomy, and meaningful choice. Our design philosophy is **Advanced inside. Simple outside.** Technology must earn its place through evidence and practical testing.

## Identity and boundaries

This Lab is separate from ClearJP, the founder's commercial Japanese AI & Localization Quality Review business. It maintains its own repository, research materials, branding, and public identity. Shared ownership does not make this research a ClearJP service or an endorsement by any cited organization.

## Current stage — problem discovery

**Mission #001, October 16, 2026:** older adults who have difficulty getting to grocery stores.

Working scope: community-dwelling adults approximately 70 and older who want to shop independently but experience difficulty making reliable grocery-store round trips. This is a research scope, not a diagnosis or an age-based assumption about ability.

**Hypothesis #001-A:** Older adults who want to choose and purchase their own groceries may be unable to travel safely and reliably to a grocery store. This may restrict their access to food, independence, and freedom of choice.

The hypothesis has **not been empirically validated by this Lab**. Mission #001 is a focused desk review, not a systematic review. No participant interviews, transport trials, app, operational service, or AI solution have been completed.

## Ethical principles

Respect adult autonomy and dignity. Preserve choices among visiting stores, supported shopping, mobile retail, delivery, and other options. Assistance must never depend on volunteering, knowledge contributions, support credits, or research-data donation. Obtain informed consent before personal-information sharing or provider contact. Publish no identifiable participant, address, financial, or medical information.

Include people who cannot use smartphones, digital interfaces, or voice systems. Human help, safe transport, and professional oversight cannot be replaced by an AI claim. Verify service availability locally before suggesting that a person can use it. Any fictional example must be explicitly labeled fictional.

## Roadmap and decision gates

1. **Now:** document evidence, assumptions, journey barriers, and a provisional priority.
2. **Next:** verify one locality's actual round-trip arrangements; prepare accessible, voluntary discovery conversations.
3. **Then:** examine lived experiences, alternatives, contradictory cases, and unmet needs. Revise or reject the problem definition if necessary.
4. **Only with supporting evidence:** define a service concept and assess whether any AI component adds value.
5. **Later:** develop and publicly demonstrate a useful, accessible prototype; evaluate safety, usability, and feasibility before exploring sustainable deployment, licensing, or commercialization.

The `service-design/` and `prototype/` folders are placeholders, not completed work. There is no fixed claim that software is necessary.

## Limitations

Japanese sources use different ages, populations, years, and measures. National estimates are not counts of our exact target group. Historical service examples are not current availability guarantees. We have not measured preferences, costs, unsafe journeys, or unmet local demand. Independence and choice are design values here; their actual meaning must be defined with participants.
