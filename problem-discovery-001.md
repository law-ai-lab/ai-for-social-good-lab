# Mission #001 — Problem Discovery

**Owner:** Rie Nakahara · **Date:** 2026-10-16 · **Version:** Problem Definition v0.1

## Research question

What prevents community-dwelling adults approximately 70 and older who want to choose and buy their own groceries from making safe, reliable store round trips, and how well do existing arrangements meet their needs?

## Selected hypothesis #001-A — H

Older adults who want to choose and purchase their own groceries may be unable to travel safely and reliably to a grocery store. This may restrict their access to food, independence, and freedom of choice.

This is an unvalidated research hypothesis. We have selected the topic, not established its prevalence or a preferred remedy.

## Problem Definition v0.1

| Element | Provisional definition |
| --- | --- |
| Who | Community-dwelling adults approximately 70+ who wish to shop independently and report difficulty completing reliable round trips. Ability, living arrangement, and support vary; living alone is not required. |
| Desired accomplishment | Choose food, quantities, quality, price, and shopping method personally, purchase it, and bring it inside the home with an acceptable level of assistance, cost, and effort. |
| Where difficulty may occur — H | Leaving home, reaching a pickup point, outbound travel, shopping, payment, arranging/boarding return transport with bags, and the final transfer inside. |
| Why alternatives may fall short — H | Routes, timing, fares, booking rules, vehicle access, carrying assistance, delivery assortment, ordering methods, or dependence on others may not match a person's needs. These are questions to investigate, not demonstrated deficiencies of named services. |
| Independence and dignity — H/value | Lost options may reduce control over what, when, and how to buy. Accepting chosen assistance can support independence; independence must not mean doing everything alone. No autonomy outcome has been measured here. |
| Still unverified — U | Target-group prevalence, preferred shopping method, frequency of failed trips, severity, costs, local coverage, and whether return travel is more consequential than other barriers. |

## Evidence synthesis — E, with limits

Source IDs resolve to original links and methods in [sources.md](research/sources.md).

- **S1:** MAFF's 2024 release estimates about **9.04 million** people met its 2020 access indicator, **25.6% of those 65+**. This is a geographic/car-access estimate, not a 2026 count of people unable to shop, nor a count of our approximately 70+ target group.
- **S2:** National municipal responses identify a continuing policy concern and existing transport and delivery measures. Municipal reports do not establish individual unmet needs or effectiveness.
- **S3:** Shopping is a common outing, while transport modes vary. This supports studying the trip in context; it does not establish that all older adults prefer store visits.
- **S4:** Observational research links perceived difficulty with individual mobility/vision, car access, and other factors. It cannot establish causes or identify our priority journey step.
- **S5–S7:** Shopping buses, mobile retail, and non-digital delivery ordering are documented alternatives. Their presence challenges any assumption that a new digital service is automatically needed.

## Challenges to our assumptions

1. **Age is not inability.** S4's selected sample largely reported shopping was easy; the sample also excluded people unable to shop alone. Both facts limit generalization.
2. **Distance is not the whole problem.** A geographic threshold cannot represent all personal abilities, routes, or support arrangements (S1, S4).
3. **Existing support may work.** S5 documents a round-trip shopping bus; S6 documents mobile retail used alongside delivery. We must look for successful arrangements and personal preferences, not only gaps.
4. **Delivery need not be digital.** S7 describes paper ordering historically. Smartphone exclusion does not prove delivery exclusion.
5. **Food access and shopping autonomy differ.** A person may have adequate food but want more choice, or may actively prefer delivery. Our research must allow both accounts.

## Priority failure point — D001, provisional

**Transition from completed purchases to boarding a feasible return journey with grocery bags.**

Working question: Can the person get themselves and their purchased groceries from the store onto a suitable return service, at an acceptable cost and time, and reach home?

Why investigate this first: it tests the complete round trip rather than treating arrival at a store as success, and makes vehicle access, waiting, timing, bags, and assistance concrete. Evidence supports examining transport and physical ability, but **does not show this transition is the most frequent or most important failure**. Selection is a researcher's provisional decision, not a confirmed user preference. No unsafe return trips are asserted.

Alternative explanations include outbound access, affordability, in-store barriers, or the last steps into the home. Change the priority if local evidence or participant accounts point elsewhere.

## Unknowns and limits — U

No locality, participant cohort, preferred store, actual schedule, fare, eligibility, boarding rule, or bag-assistance policy has been selected or verified. We do not know whether failures occur, how often, or whether non-use reflects preference, lack of information, or service inadequacy. Historical reports cannot establish current service access. No interviews, participant statistics, or service outcomes were generated by the Lab.

## Next validation step — 30–60 minutes

Conduct **one locality's public-document return-journey audit**, using a real shopping bus, community bus, or demand-responsive transport arrangement. This tests service constraints, not user preferences.

1. 0–10 minutes: select a locality with official current transport information and a grocery destination; record why it was chosen. Do not use any resident's home address.
2. 10–30 minutes: record document dates, route/pickup area, outbound and return times or booking conditions, fares, eligibility, booking channel, walking/boarding requirements, and published bag/assistance rules. Mark absent details unknown; do not invent a connection.
3. 30–45 minutes: assess whether an illustrative store visit fits both directions. Label it **fictional route-planning example**, not a participant journey or a safety certification. Test a missed return or longer shopping time if the published rules permit analysis.
4. 45–60 minutes: publish a short audit with links, contradictions, and unresolved questions. Retain, revise, or replace D001 based on what it shows.

After the audit, prepare voluntary discovery conversations with people who experience difficulty **and** people whose arrangements work. Start with: “Please walk me through your most recent grocery shopping trip, from leaving home to bringing the groceries inside.” Ask what worked, what they chose, and what they would change before suggesting solutions. Follow [consent and privacy safeguards](ethics-privacy/principles.md); do not recruit or contact providers without appropriate authorization/consent.

**Decision gate:** No software features until concrete experiences and service evidence justify them. A document audit alone cannot validate the hypothesis or an AI solution.
